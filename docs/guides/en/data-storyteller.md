# Telling the Story (data-storyteller)

**What it does:** structures your presentation or report so the audience
follows it, cares about it, and knows exactly what you're asking them to do.

**Use it when:** you have findings and need a slide deck, an executive
summary, or a report that *convinces* someone — not just shows numbers.

## A realistic use case

Churn doubled after your pricing change, but revenue per account is up 20%.
You have ten minutes with the exec team and you need them to approve budget
for a win-back campaign.

## Try it now (copy-paste and edit)

```
/dataink:data-storyteller Churn doubled after our pricing change, but revenue
per account is up 20%. I have 10 minutes with the exec team and I need them
to approve a $150k win-back campaign. Help me structure the presentation.
```

## What happens next

1. Claude first pins down the basics: who's in the room, how much time you
   have, and what decision you need from them. Busy executives get the
   recommendation *first*, evidence after — not a slow build-up.
2. It makes you compress your whole ask into one sentence (the "Big Idea").
   If you can't, that's the real problem, and it helps you fix it before any
   slides exist.
3. It shapes the deck like a story: here's the situation → here's what it's
   costing us to do nothing → here's the fix and the payoff → here's the ask.
   Your audience is the main character; the data is the supporting evidence.
4. Every slide gets a title that states a point ("Churn is erasing the pricing
   gains"), never a topic ("Churn Overview").
5. Then it checks the skeleton: read only the slide titles, top to bottom — do
   they tell the complete story on their own? If not, it restructures before
   you polish anything.

## How to know you got a good one

- Someone reading only your slide titles understands the whole argument.
- The last slide asks for something specific ("approve $150k by Friday"), not
  "questions?".
- Every chart on every slide supports that slide's title — anything that
  doesn't gets moved to the appendix.

## The mistake it saves you from

Building the slides first and looking for the story afterwards. Structure
first, charts second — it's faster and the result is sharper.
